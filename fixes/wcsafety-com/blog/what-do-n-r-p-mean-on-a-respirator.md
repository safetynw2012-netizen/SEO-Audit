If you have ever picked up a disposable mask or a half-mask cartridge and
seen a code like **N95**, **R95**, or **P100** stamped on it, you have run
into the United States respirator rating system. Those letters and numbers
are not marketing — they are defined by NIOSH (the National Institute for
Occupational Safety and Health) under federal regulation **42 CFR Part 84**,
and they tell you two specific things about the filter.

This guide explains exactly what the **N**, **R**, and **P** letter means,
what the **95 / 99 / 100** number means, and how to use both to pick the
right filter for the job. If you only need a fast recommendation rather than
the full explanation, our
[N95 vs. KN95 vs. P100 comparison](/blogs/guides/n95-vs-kn95-vs-p100-which-respirator-do-you-actually-need)
gets you to an answer quickly.

## The short answer

A NIOSH filter rating has two parts: a **letter** and a **number**.

- The **letter (N, R, or P)** describes how well the filter holds up
  against **oil-based** particles and aerosols.
- The **number (95, 99, or 100)** is the **minimum filtration
  efficiency** against the hardest-to-catch particle size.

Put them together and you get nine official particulate-filter classes:
**N95, N99, N100, R95, R99, R100, P95, P99, P100**.

## What the letter means: oil resistance

The letter exists because **oil aerosols degrade some filter media**. Many
particulate filters trap fine particles using an electrostatic charge on
the fibers. Oil mist (from cutting fluids, lubricants, glycerin-based
aerosols, some pesticides, and similar sources) can strip that charge over
time and let efficiency drop. NIOSH created three classes to handle this:

| Letter | Meaning | Use with oil aerosols? | Typical service limit in oil |
| ------ | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **N**  | **N**ot resistant to oil       | No — non-oil particulates only            | Not rated for oil at all                                |
| **R**  | **R**esistant to oil           | Yes, but time-restricted                  | NIOSH limits R-series to a single shift / up to 8 hours of oil exposure |
| **P**  | Oil-**P**roof (strongly resistant) | Yes                                   | Follow the manufacturer's stated service life (often expressed as a use-hour or 30-day limit) |

Plain-language version:

- **N** — "**N**o oil." Fine for dust, wood, fibreglass, pollen, welding
  fume, most biological particles, drywall, concrete, and the like — as
  long as there is **no oil mist** in the air. The everyday N95 is in
  this group.
- **R** — "**R**esistant." Can be used where oil aerosols are present,
  but only for a **limited time** (NIOSH restricts R-series filters to a
  single use or up to one 8-hour shift in oil-containing atmospheres).
- **P** — "Oil-**P**roof." The most oil-tolerant. Can be used in oil
  atmospheres for the manufacturer's full stated service life. P-series
  filters such as the
  [3M 2091 P100 filter](/products/3m-2091-p100-respirator-filter)
  and combination cartridges like the
  [3M 60921 P100 organic-vapor respirator cartridge](/products/3m-60921-p100-organic-vapor-respirator-cartridge)
  are popular precisely because you do not have to ask "is there oil in
  this air?" before using them.

If you are not sure whether oil aerosols are present, **err toward P**.
The cost difference is small and a P-rated filter is valid everywhere an
N- or R-rated one is.

## What the number means: filtration efficiency

The number is the **minimum percentage of particles the filter captures**,
measured by NIOSH at the *most penetrating particle size* — roughly
**0.3 microns**, the size that is hardest to filter. Particles both larger
**and** smaller than 0.3 µm are actually captured *more* efficiently, which
is a common point of confusion.

| Number | Minimum efficiency | Equivalent |
| ------ | ------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| **95** | ≥ 95%              | The familiar baseline (N95)       |
| **99** | ≥ 99%              | Step up from 95                   |
| **100**| ≥ 99.97%           | HEPA-equivalent (P100, N100, R100)|

So a **P100** filter is "oil-proof, ≥ 99.97% efficient" — the highest
NIOSH particulate class. A pink/magenta filter color is the industry
convention for P100, which is why so many half-mask cartridges look pink.

## Putting the letter and number together

Read the code left to right:

- **N95** = not oil-resistant, ≥ 95% efficient. The default disposable
  respirator. See our roundup of the
  [best N95 respirators for 2026](/blogs/guides/best-n95-respirators-2026).
- **R95** = oil-resistant (time-limited), ≥ 95% efficient. Uncommon in
  retail; mostly specialty industrial use.
- **P100** = oil-proof, ≥ 99.97% efficient. The workhorse for
  abatement, metalworking, and chemical work. Browse half-mask options in

  collection, or grab-and-go disposables in

## What N, R, and P do NOT tell you

This is the part people get wrong most often.

1. **They are particulate ratings only.** A P100 filter stops
   particles. It does **not** protect you from gases or vapors —
   solvents, paint fumes, ammonia, chlorine, and so on. Those require a
   **chemical cartridge** (color-coded by hazard; e.g., black =
   organic vapor) or a **combination** cartridge that pairs a P100
   particulate stage with a gas/vapor stage, like the
   [3M 60921 organic-vapor + P100 cartridge](/products/3m-60921-p100-organic-vapor-respirator-cartridge).
2. **They are not a protection factor.** How much a respirator reduces
   your exposure (the *Assigned Protection Factor*) is set by OSHA based
   on the **facepiece type** — a filtering facepiece or half mask is
   APF 10, a full facepiece is APF 50 — not by the filter letter. For
   the regulatory side of who sets what, see our
   [NIOSH vs. OSHA explainer](/blogs/guides/breakdown-of-niosh-vs-osha-what-do-they-do).
3. **They do not equal KN95 or FFP2.** N/R/P is specifically the **US
   NIOSH 42 CFR 84** system. KN95 (China GB 2626) and FFP1/FFP2/FFP3
   (Europe EN 149) are separate standards with their own tests. They are
   roughly comparable in efficiency but are **not** interchangeable
   certifications — the
   [N95 vs. KN95 vs. P100 guide](/blogs/guides/n95-vs-kn95-vs-p100-which-respirator-do-you-actually-need)
   breaks down the differences.
4. **They do not guarantee fit.** Even a perfect P100 filter protects
   nothing if air leaks around the seal. A respirator only performs at
   its rating with a proper seal and (for workplace use) a passed fit
   test.

## How to verify a respirator is really NIOSH-rated

Genuine NIOSH-approved respirators carry, on the filtering facepiece or
cartridge and on the packaging:

- The word **NIOSH** (correctly spelled — counterfeits frequently
  misspell it).
- The filter class — e.g., **N95**, **P100**.
- An approval number in the form **TC-84A-XXXX**.
- The manufacturer name or registered logo, and the model/lot number.

If any of these are missing, treat the product as unverified. NIOSH
publishes the Certified Equipment List so an approval number can be
checked against the official database — this cross-checking step is part
of our [editorial methodology](/pages/editorial-methodology).

## Choosing the right rating: a quick decision path

1. **Gases or vapors in the air?** If yes, you need a chemical or
   combination cartridge — a bare N/R/P filter is not enough. Half-mask
   platforms such as those in the
   [best half-face respirator](/blogs/news/best-half-face-respirator)
   roundup accept combination cartridges.
2. **Oil mist or oil aerosol present?** If yes (or unknown), choose
   **P**. If definitely no oil, **N** is fine and usually cheapest.
3. **How clean does the air need to be?** Routine dust → **95**.
   High-consequence particles (lead, asbestos abatement, silica,
   pharmaceutical) → **100**.
4. **Reusable or disposable?** Disposable filtering facepieces for
   short, low-maintenance tasks; cartridge-based half or full masks
   when you want to swap filters and reuse the facepiece. If you go
   reusable, read [how to properly clean a respirator safely](/blogs/news/how-to-properly-clean-a-respirator-safely).

## When to replace a filter

Discard or change a filter when **any** of these is true:

- Breathing through it becomes noticeably harder (particulate loading).
- It is physically damaged, wet, crushed, or visibly soiled.
- You smell or taste contaminant (for combination cartridges — the
  gas/vapor stage is exhausted).
- The **R-series** time-in-oil limit (one shift / ~8 hours) is reached.
- The manufacturer's stated service life is reached, whichever comes
  first.

When in doubt, replace it. A filter is the cheapest part of the system.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is P100 better than N95?**
For particulate filtration, yes — P100 is oil-proof and ≥ 99.97%
efficient versus N95's non-oil, ≥ 95%. But "better" depends on the job:
N95 is lighter, cheaper, and fully adequate where there is no oil and the
hazard does not demand 99.97%.

**Does N95 filter viruses?**
N95 captures ≥ 95% of 0.3 µm particles, and respiratory particles
carrying pathogens are generally filtered efficiently by an N95 *when it
is sealed properly*. Fit matters as much as the rating.

**What does the "95" actually represent?**
A minimum of 95% efficiency at the most-penetrating particle size
(~0.3 µm) under NIOSH's standardized test — not "95% of all particles
of all sizes," and not a marketing figure.

**Why is my P100 filter pink?**
Magenta/pink is the long-standing industry color convention for P100. It
is a visual shortcut, not part of the legal rating.

**Can I use one respirator for both dust and paint fumes?**
Only with a **combination** cartridge (particulate + organic-vapor
stages). A standalone N/R/P filter does nothing against solvent vapor.
